# Design

## Why it's built this way

Sorting sources into primary and secondary is the entry point, not the goal. The game climbs through the historical
thinking skills a historian uses on a document (sourcing, close reading, corroboration, perspective), one shift at a
time, the way *Papers, Please* adds a new rule each day.

1. **Stamp it.** Primary or secondary, with proof. The label (who, when, what) counts as proof: reading it first is
   the skill.
2. **Catalogue cards.** Who made it, when, were they there, who was it for. Filled with words from the notebook.
3. **Questions from a researcher.** Multiple choice where the answer is not enough: point at the line that proves it.
   Includes reading an opinion against the facts in the same source, and two sources that disagree.
4. **The twist.** The same page is primary for one question and secondary for another. A primary source can hide
   inside a secondary one. A retelling of an eyewitness is still the eyewitness.
5. **The case board.** A *Golden Idol* deduction: fill the blanks with collected words; the game only says whether
   two or fewer are wrong.

Mistakes cost a seal and come back as a slip "in the mail" at the end of the shift. Nothing times the student.

## Ideas not built yet

- **Compare**: two accounts side by side; pin the claims that disagree, then choose which to trust and why.
- **Anachronism**: a "1605 letter" that mentions something that can't be there. These documents are invented, so the
  game must say so once the student catches it. Never mix invented documents into the stamp pile without that.
- **Hotspots** for images and objects (Grade 7: Göbekli Tepe pillars, cave paintings, cuneiform, Ötzi): point at part
  of a picture instead of a sentence. `doc.js` already shows scans; a hotspot is a ref to a rectangle.
- **Teacher view**: a results code or class sheet at the end (seals per task, which hints were needed).
- **British North America**: Loyalist diaries, the Durham Report, Confederation debates and newspapers. Plenty of
  disagreeing accounts for "compare".
- **A later textbook** as a source (for example a 1900s school history): secondary about 1604, primary about how
  people were taught in its own time. Needs a real, public-domain book.
